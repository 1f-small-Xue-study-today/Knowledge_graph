Flow Matching（流匹配） learns a time-dependent velocity field for a generative ordinary differential equation, or ODE.

01 / The basic idea

Learn a map of velocities, not a list of destinations.

Imagine particles initially scattered like Gaussian noise. At every location and time, a velocity field says which direction to move and how fast. Following that field rearranges the whole cloud into a data distribution.

Noise x₀ ∼ p₀→dx/dt = vθ(x,t)→Data-like sample x₁
Time convention: here t = 0 means noise and t = 1 means data. In our DDPM lesson, x₀ meant clean data and xT meant noise. The symbols have changed roles; always check the time convention before comparing formulas.

We will use a simple and common construction: pair a noise sample x₀ with a data sample x₁, connect them by a straight line, and train on its velocity. Flow Matching supports other probability paths too; it is not restricted to straight interpolation.

Training

We know both endpoints. Construct an intermediate point and its target velocity. Regress a neural network toward that velocity.

Generation

We know only a new noise sample. Query the trained network repeatedly and solve the ODE from t = 0 to t = 1. No target endpoint is supplied.

02 / Interactive · one training path

A straight line gives an easy velocity target.

xₜ = (1−t)x₀ + t x₁,   t ∈ [0,1]
uₜ = dxₜ/dt = x₁ − x₀

The derivative is the displacement over a unit time interval. For one endpoint pair, it is constant. The network’s predicted velocity will generally depend on both location and time.

Try: keep the endpoints fixed and move t. Position changes; target velocity does not. Move the data endpoint to the left of the noise endpoint: velocity becomes negative.

This is a hand-controlled training example. No network is trained by these sliders.

With x₀ = −1 and x₁ = 2, the target velocity is 3. At t = 0.4, xₜ = 0.2. A small Euler step of size 0.1 with the correct velocity gives 0.2+0.1×3 = 0.5.

03 / The learning objective

Learn the average velocity appropriate here and now.

L = E over x₀,x₁,t [‖vθ(xₜ,t) − (x₁−x₀)‖²]

For this independent-pair construction, sample x₀ ∼ N(0,I) and x₁ from the dataset independently, then sample t uniformly. The network receives xₜ and t—not either endpoint. Squared error teaches it to predict the target velocity.

But many paths can pass through the same point.

Consider two possible training pairs: −2 → 2 has velocity +4; 2 → −2 has velocity −4. At t = 0.5, both pass through x = 0. A network given only (0,0.5) cannot identify which pair we secretly sampled.

v*(x,t) = E[x₁−x₀ | xₜ = x, t]

The squared-error optimum is the conditional average velocity. If the two crossing paths above are equally likely, the best prediction at their crossing is zero, with average squared error 16. A nonzero training loss can reflect genuine ambiguity rather than a bad predictor.

Straight training paths do not imply that generated trajectories are straight. Generation follows the averaged velocity field, not one known endpoint pair. The two-path crossing example is intuition for regression, not a guarantee of a smooth ODE for a discrete collapsing distribution.

What does “conditional” mean here?

Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) constructs easy velocity targets by conditioning on sampled endpoints or another latent variable. That training construction can produce an unconditional generator. Separately, an observation-conditioned generator vθ(x,t|o) receives a prompt or robot observation o. These are two different uses of “conditional.”

Experiment 1: choose Euler with 1 step and inspect the end. Every point collapses to zero! At t = 0 the exact velocity is −x, so the single update x ← x+(−x) loses all spread. The field is correct, but the integration is too coarse.

Experiment 2: increase to 40 or 100 steps, then compare Euler and Heun. The two groups emerge. Heun spends twice as many field evaluations per step; equal step counts are not equal computational cost.

Experiment 3: move the trajectory slider back to t = 0 and sweep forward. Watch the distribution narrow first, then separate. Independent straight-line pairing does not promise a straight or uniformly expanding marginal flow.

The numerical updates

Euler: xnext = x + Δt vθ(x,t)
Heun: k₁ = vθ(x,t),   xtrial = x + Δt k₁
k₂ = vθ(xtrial,t+Δt)
xnext = x + (Δt/2)(k₁+k₂)

Heun averages the slope at the beginning and at a predicted endpoint. Under suitable smoothness, Euler has first-order global accuracy and Heun second-order accuracy. More accurate integration cannot fix an incorrectly learned velocity field.

Where does the analytic velocity in this lab come from?

Let the target component mean be m = ±2 and its variance s² = 0.35². The interpolated component has mean tm and variance V = (1−t)²+t²s². The covariance between endpoint displacement (x₁−x₀) and xₜ within that component is C = ts²−(1−t). Gaussian conditioning gives velocity m+(C/V)(x−tm).

Average this over the posterior component probabilities. With equal mixture weights, the posterior average of m is M = 2 tanh(2tx/V). The final marginal velocity is v(x,t) = M+(C/V)(x−tM). Since V stays positive, it is well-defined even at the endpoints for this toy model.

For a fixed initial noise sample, fixed velocity field, and fixed solver, this ODE sampler is deterministic. Diversity comes from drawing different initial noise. This statement is about the standard ODE construction here; other generative samplers can add stochasticity.

04b / Interactive · see the velocity field

A field is a rule that gives a velocity everywhere.

A particle has a position. A velocity field gives the direction and speed a particle would have at any position and time. The field exists even where no particle currently sits. A trajectory is what you get by repeatedly following that rule.

State r = (x,y),   v(r,t) = (vₓ,vᵧ)
dr/dt = v(r,t)
Small step: (x,y)next ≈ (x,y) + Δt(vₓ,vᵧ)

The field → arrows

Place an imaginary particle anywhere on the highlighted time slice. Its arrow tells you its instantaneous direction. Longer arrows mean greater speed, except where explicitly capped for readability.

The system → trajectories

The field is the right-hand side of the ODE. An initial state plus that rule determines a trajectory. The model parameters stay fixed while the state changes during generation.

This new toy example has two data coordinates. Initially x and y are independent standard Gaussians. The target has two groups along x, as before, and a narrower Gaussian along y with standard deviation 0.6. This extension uses an analytic velocity field so the mechanism is visible.

Rotate the space–time view

Axes: x, y, and flow time t. The third axis is time, not a third spatial coordinate. Orange arrows lie within the selected time plane and represent (vₓ,vᵧ). The upward component of a space–time trajectory is dt/dt = 1, so its full tangent is (vₓ,vᵧ,1).

Drag to rotate, or use the rotation sliders with a keyboard. Teal dots: particles at the selected time. Faint curves: trajectories. Orange arrows: local field. Purple dot and arrow: your probe. Arrow length = 0.18 × speed in data coordinates, capped at 0.65; the readout shows the uncapped value.

Try 1: set t = 0. Arrows point inward because this independent-pair field initially has v = (−x,−y). Move to later times: the x component helps separate the two groups.

Try 2: keep the purple probe at (1,1), then change time. The velocity changes even though its location stays fixed. That is what a time-dependent field means.

Try 3: follow one faint trajectory upward. At each time its motion within the x–y plane agrees with the local field. Particles do not choose and memorize an arrow once; the velocity is re-evaluated along the path.

How is the extra y coordinate modeled?

It is independent of x in this toy model. For a standard Gaussian source and N(0,0.6²) target with independent straight-line endpoint pairing, Vᵧ(t) = (1−t)²+0.6²t². The marginal velocity is vᵧ(y,t) = [0.6²t−(1−t)]y/Vᵧ(t). The x component is the same mixture velocity used above. Paths are integrated with 100 Heun steps.

A real network approximates this vector-valued function from training examples. Its input is the current state and time; its output is a vector with the same number of coordinates as the state. These arrows are velocities, not forces or probabilities. For an image, the state may have hundreds of thousands of coordinates; this plot makes a two-coordinate example visible.
05 / Why matching velocities moves distributions

Local motion changes the whole probability cloud.

Think of probability as conserved mass carried by moving particles. The density pₜ and velocity field vₜ are connected by the continuity equation:

∂pₜ/∂t + ∇·(pₜ vₜ) = 0

The divergence ∇· measures net outward flow locally. More outward probability flow lowers local density; inward flow raises it. This is not the same operation as KL divergence, which compares two distributions.

For the chosen endpoint coupling and interpolating paths, averaging conditional velocities at each (x,t) yields a velocity compatible with their marginal densities. With suitable regularity, an exact velocity and exact ODE integration transport the source distribution along those densities to the target. Learned approximation and numerical error weaken that ideal result.

Where are Jensen, KL, and ELBO?

The basic Flow Matching regression objective can be constructed without an ELBO or explicit KL penalty. Its justification uses probability transport and conditional expectations. Do not assume every generative training objective must be “reconstruction plus KL.” Flow Matching can train continuous normalizing flows; evaluating likelihood then involves additional density-change calculations, which basic sample generation does not need.

Is independent pairing optimal transport?

No. A coupling specifies how noise endpoints and data endpoints are paired. Independent pairing is easy but can create crossing training paths and large regression ambiguity. Optimal-transport-inspired pairings can reduce transport cost under chosen assumptions. Straight interpolation by itself does not make an arbitrary coupling globally optimal transport or guarantee one-step generation.

These are the specific formulations in these lessons, not a strict division of all methods. Diffusion models can use deterministic ODE samplers and velocity parameterizations; Flow Matching supports diffusion probability paths too. Neither name alone guarantees faster inference or better samples.

From random samples to robot actions

Let x be an entire proposed action sequence and o the current observation. Training pairs a demonstration action sequence x₁ with noise x₀, then learns vθ(xₜ,t|o). At inference, hold o fixed during a flow solve, integrate from noise to a proposed action sequence, and execute according to the controller’s policy.

Flow time t is not physical execution time. One vector can contain actions at several future physical timestamps. Solving the flow refines that entire vector. Safety, feasibility, closed-loop feedback, and replanning are separate concerns; the generative ODE alone provides no control-stability guarantee.

07 / Algorithm and active recall

A short training loop, then an ODE solver.

# Independent-pair conditional flow-matching training
x1 = sample_real_data()
x0 = standard_normal_like(x1)
t = uniform(0, 1, per_example=True)
xt = (1 - t) * x0 + t * x1
target_velocity = x1 - x0
prediction = network(xt, t)
loss = mean((prediction - target_velocity)**2)
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()

# Euler generation: no true data endpoint is available
x = standard_normal(data_shape)
dt = 1 / number_of_steps
for k in range(number_of_steps):
    t = k * dt
    x = x + dt * network(x, t)
return x

Broadcast t over all feature dimensions. For observation conditioning, give the same observation to the network alongside xt and t during training, and at every solver step during generation. The standard regression training loop does not integrate the ODE for every minibatch.

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the target velocity: for this convention it is x₁−x₀, not x₀−x₁.
  • Giving the network x₁ as an input in an unconditional generator. That endpoint is not available at generation.
  • Forgetting Δt in Euler updates, or mixing opposite time conventions.
  • Assuming target velocity is the same as predicted noise or predicted clean data.
  • Expecting constant target velocity on one training pair to imply a constant learned field.
  • Comparing solvers only by steps without counting network evaluations.
1. If x₀ = −2, x₁ = 1, t = 0.25, what are xₜ and the target velocity?

xₜ = 0.75(−2)+0.25(1) = −1.25. The target velocity is 1−(−2) = 3.

2. Why can a well-trained network have nonzero velocity MSE?

Several endpoint pairs can lead to the same intermediate location and time with different target velocities. The optimum predicts their conditional average; the remaining conditional variance is irreducible from those inputs alone.

3. If conditional paths are straight, why can sampling curves bend?

The ODE follows the marginal, averaged velocity field. As a particle moves, its location and time change which endpoint pairs are plausible, so the average direction can change.

4. Does the ODE receive a destination sample?

No. It receives the current generated state and time, optionally an external condition. Only the training construction knows the endpoint data sample.

5. Where does randomness enter this ODE generator?

In the initial sample x₀. Given that sample and fixed model/solver, the trajectory is deterministic.

6. Does straight interpolation imply optimal transport?

No. The coupling also matters. Independently paired endpoints are not generally a minimum-cost coupling.

7. What should I learn next?

First explain endpoint pairing, conditional-average velocity, and an Euler step without notes. Then implement a small velocity network on a two-dimensional toy dataset, compare its learned field with numerical trajectories, and study conditioning and alternative couplings.
