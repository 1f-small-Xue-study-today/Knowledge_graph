01 / The mathematical meaning

A score is a gradient—not a rating.

s(x) = ∇ₓ log p(x)

p(x) is a density. log p(x) is a scalar landscape. Its gradient s(x) is a vector pointing in the direction of steepest local increase in log density. In one dimension it is simply the slope d log p(x)/dx.

Density p(x)
how mass is distributed→ log →Log-density landscape→ gradient →Score s(x)
direction and slope

The derivative is with respect to the data x. It is not the parameter gradient ∇θ used to update a network, nor the parameter score sometimes used in statistical estimation.

A Gaussian gives the simplest example

p(x) = N(μ,τ²)
log p(x) = constant − (x−μ)²/(2τ²)
s(x) = −(x−μ)/τ²

For μ = 2 and τ = 1: at x = 0 the score is +2, at x = 3 it is −1, and at x = 2 it is zero. The arrows point toward the center. A narrower Gaussian has a steeper score at the same displacement.

A zero score only means a stationary point. It can be a maximum, a minimum, or a saddle—not necessarily a good sample. A symmetric two-peak density has score zero in the low-density valley between its peaks.

Why use log density?

If p(x) = p̃(x)/Z and Z does not depend on x, then ∇ₓ log p(x) = ∇ₓ log p̃(x): the unknown normalization constant disappears. On a connected domain with a positive smooth density, the exact score determines log density up to an additive constant; normalization then fixes that constant.

03 / Denoising score matching

We know the corruption even when we don’t know the data density.

We usually have samples from the data distribution, not a formula for p(x). So we cannot directly label training examples with ∇ log p(x). Gaussian corruption gives a target we can calculate:

x̃ = x + σ ε,   ε ∼ N(0,I)
qσ(x̃|x) = N(x,σ²I)
∇x̃ log qσ(x̃|x) = −(x̃−x)/σ² = −ε/σ

Draw clean x, noise ε, and a positive noise level σ. The network sees x̃ and σ and predicts a vector of the same shape as x̃.

LDSM = E [λ(σ) ‖sθ(x̃,σ) + ε/σ‖²]

λ(σ) > 0 controls how noise levels are weighted; λ = σ² is a common choice for this additive-noise parameterization. This is denoising score matching, or DSM. It does not require an explicit KL penalty or ELBO in its basic training loop.

Conditional target versus marginal score

The label −ε/σ points toward the particular clean sample used to construct this example. But several clean samples can explain the same noisy input. Squared-error regression learns the conditional average of those labels:

s*(x̃,σ) = E[−ε/σ | x̃,σ] = ∇x̃ log pσ(x̃)

Here pσ is the noise-smoothed data distribution, obtained by averaging the corruption density over clean data. The average conditional score equals this marginal score under suitable regularity. An ideal model need not predict every sampled label exactly.

Work through one label

Take x = 2, σ = 0.5, ε = −1. Then x̃ = 1.5 and the label −ε/σ = +2. It says “move right” for this pair. The network receives (1.5, 0.5), not the hidden clean x = 2.

For additive Gaussian noise, the posterior mean denoiser satisfies E[x|x̃] = x̃ + σ²s(x̃,σ). This is a mean estimate, not a draw from the posterior and not generally the original sample.

04 / Interactive · sampling is not optimization

Climbing to peaks is not enough to sample a distribution.

Following the score without noise tends to move particles into stationary points, often density maxima. A collection of points concentrated at the peaks does not reproduce the distribution’s width. Langevin dynamics combines score drift with random motion:

xnext = x + h s(x) + √(2h) ξ,   ξ ∼ N(0,I)

This is the unadjusted Langevin update with our step-size convention. The continuous-time process dX = s(X)dτ + √2 dW has p as an invariant density under appropriate conditions. A finite step size introduces bias, and a finite run may not mix across separated modes.

Top teal: deterministic score ascent · Bottom blue: Langevin · Orange curves: target density. Both start from the same 1,000 N(0,3²) samples. Changing target σ or h resets both runs. Histogram bars are clipped at density 1.2; peak collapse can be taller.

Try: advance 300 steps. The top distribution becomes too concentrated, while Langevin retains spread. Reset and use a smaller h: you need more steps for the same elapsed sampling time τ = steps × h.

This lab uses a known analytic score, not a trained model. Its Langevin chain targets the selected fixed pσ. At σ > 0 that target is still noisy data. This demonstration alone is not a complete noise-to-data generative pipeline.
05 / Learn a family of smoothed scores

Start broad, then recover detail.

At high noise, fine structure is smoothed out and separated modes overlap more. At low noise, sharp detail returns. A noise-conditioned score network learns sθ(x,σ) across a collection of positive σ values.

Large σ
broad exploration→Intermediate σ
find structure→Small σ
refine samples

Annealed Langevin sampling runs several Langevin steps at each noise level, then decreases σ and uses the updated score. Step sizes must be chosen in relation to the noise scale. A finite schedule ending at positive σ only approximates the clean target; optional denoising and model error also affect the result.

Large-noise initialization is often a broad Gaussian approximation when noise dominates the data scale. It is not automatically an exact sample from pσmax. Neither smoothing nor more steps guarantees good mixing in every problem.

Our time-independent Langevin lab lets you inspect individual noise levels. A full annealed run changes its target density over stages; it is a separate procedure from holding σ fixed.

06 / Connect DDPM and Flow Matching

The score is part of a sampler’s rule, not always the whole velocity.

DDPM: noise prediction encodes a score

In DDPM, xₜ = √ᾱₜ x₀ + √(1−ᾱₜ)ε. The ideal conditional-mean noise predictor gives the noisy marginal score:

sθ(xₜ,t) = −εθ(xₜ,t) / √(1−ᾱₜ)

This scaling applies to the variance-preserving corruption above. For additive corruption x̃ = x+σε, use −εθ/σ. Do not interchange schedules or their scaling factors.

Continuous-time diffusion: forward and reverse SDEs

Use diffusion time t increasing from clean data at 0 to noise at T. For a scalar diffusion coefficient g(t) that depends only on time:

Forward: dX = f(X,t) dt + g(t) dW
Reverse-time: dX = [f(X,t) − g(t)²s(X,t)] dt + g(t) dW̄
Probability-flow ODE: dX = [f(X,t) − ½g(t)²s(X,t)] dt

For reverse generation these last two equations are integrated from T down to 0, so dt is negative. A discrete reverse stochastic step uses noise magnitude g√|dt|. The reverse-time Brownian term must be interpreted in that backward-time convention.

With the exact score and suitable regularity, the probability-flow ODE and the diffusion SDE have the same one-time marginal densities; they do not have the same individual trajectories or path distributions. Numerical solvers and learned scores introduce error.

Notice the drift f and the coefficient multiplying the score. A score is a log-density gradient, while a velocity or drift specifies a particular dynamics. Setting velocity equal to score alone generally does not implement the probability-flow ODE.

Relation to Flow Matching

Flow Matching directly regresses a velocity compatible with chosen probability paths. Score models estimate log-density gradients and combine them with a specified sampler. Their families overlap: Flow Matching can use diffusion paths, and score models can generate with an ODE. The field you learned about earlier can represent either quantity, but its mathematical meaning must be stated.

07 / Implementation and active recall

Match each operation with its meaning.

# Denoising score matching: additive Gaussian corruption
x = sample_real_data()
sigma = sample_positive_noise_level(per_example=True)
eps = standard_normal_like(x)
noisy = x + sigma * eps
target = -eps / sigma
prediction = score_network(noisy, sigma)
# Example weighting lambda(sigma) = sigma**2
loss = mean(sigma**2 * (prediction - target)**2)
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()

# Fixed-noise-level Langevin steps (one stage of annealing)
for k in range(number_of_steps):
    x = x + h * score_network(x, sigma) + sqrt(2*h) * normal_like(x)

Broadcast σ over data coordinates. Sample it strictly above zero for this loss. During training the network parameters receive gradients; during sampling the frozen network produces a data-space score vector. Do not accidentally compute a parameter gradient and call that the sample’s score.

1. For N(2, 0.25), what is the score at x = 1?

The second argument is variance, so s(1) = −(1−2)/0.25 = 4. It points right.

2. Does score zero imply high probability?

No. It also occurs at valleys and saddles. In our low-noise symmetric mixture, x = 0 is a low-density stationary point.

3. Why doesn’t deterministic score ascent reproduce the target?

It can concentrate trajectories at maxima instead of reproducing the correct probability mass around them. Langevin’s random motion counterbalances the drift under the appropriate dynamics.

4. What score does denoising score matching learn?

The marginal score of the noise-smoothed data pσ, not the conditional score of each individual training pair. Squared error averages the conditional labels over plausible clean origins.

5. With σ = 0.2 and ε = 0.5, what is the label?

−ε/σ = −2.5. The variance is σ² = 0.04; the equivalent expression is −(x̃−x)/σ².

6. Is a finite-step Langevin run exact sampling?

No. There can be discretization bias, insufficient mixing, initialization error, and learned-score error. In this lab only the last error is absent because the score is analytic.

7. Are the score and Flow Matching velocity interchangeable?

No. A score describes a log-density gradient. A velocity describes a transport dynamics. A specific probability-flow ODE combines score, drift, and diffusion coefficients to produce a velocity.
